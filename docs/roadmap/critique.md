# Adversarial critique (workflow)

## Ranked features

### Eval harness (runEval.ts) — NOT one of Ben's asks, but the true prerequisite
**build now** · expected value: high

Precision is the entire asset with this audience and it is currently unmeasured; repeat agreement is 71% self-consistency, not correctness. Every other feature adds finding surface area in an unmeasurable direction. The fixtures already exist in packages/eval; only runEval.ts is missing. Nothing else should ship first.

### Feature 4 — synthetic demo documents (510(k)s, DHFs, CAPAs, FDA correspondence)
**build now** · expected value: medium

The only ask that directly serves the real goal — demos that win a second customer. Cheapest to build, no new finding surface, no licensing or severity landmines. It is under-designed precisely because it is unglamorous, which is the scope error in miniature.

### Feature 3 — cumulative / continuous risk (Cumulative Change Ledger)
**reshape** · expected value: high

Highest validated customer pain (catch-up 510(k)/LTF creep, enforcement-backed, felt by a real manufacturer like Fresenius). But phases 4-5 depend on feature 1's graph and baseline identity that do not exist. Ship only the deterministic wedge — Appendix B comparison table and the GP7 wrong-comparator check, both code-computable and citable — and refuse the verdict. Kill the free-text 'evaluate my risk' box Ben asked for.

### Feature 1 — spider/mind map of references and risk relationships
**build later** · expected value: medium

Most demo-forward, most likely to fail in front of a customer. Day-one edges do not exist for a new corpus; the valuable output (absence findings) produces false-alarm storms on partial corpora and is unshippable without the completeness gate, which is unmeasurable without the harness. A convincing wrong picture is worse than none. Defer until the harness and deterministic edge extraction exist.

### Feature 2 — public best-practices corpus near SOPs
**cut** · expected value: low

Premise is legally false by the team's own research; the salvage (FDA warning-letter frequency mining) is real but belongs as narrow severity infrastructure for feature 3, not a SOPs-adjacent UI section. No evidence the validated customer wants it. Ships copyrighted-license risk (ODbL) to a paying customer before counsel sign-off. Keep only the frequency mining, later; cut the tab, the product-code expansion, and the CI license theater for now.

## Kill shots

1. Sequencing inversion: all three features add finding surface area on top of an unmeasured, 71%-self-consistent base, when precision is the stated whole asset. runEval.ts does not exist (verified: packages/eval has only types.ts + CLIs) and appears in none of the four plans. Every design's own risk section admits it is 'unmeasurable until the eval harness exists' — they route around the harness five times instead of building it once.

2. Feature 1's flagship output is structurally unshippable to a real customer: absence findings (orphan requirement, unverified control) fire falsely when a customer uploads a partial corpus — 40 of 400 docs makes everything look orphaned — producing exactly the false-alarm storm the README says destroys credibility, with no measured gate to stop it.

3. Feature 1 has no day-one data for a NEW customer: the only real edges are conflicts_with (which requires a prior paid cross-check review with hand-enumerated related IDs) plus doc-ID regex. A fresh upload draws an empty or hairball map. 'Demoable day one' is true only for documents you already paid to review.

4. Feature 3's anchoring guard is theater: anchoring a change-fact to the engineer's just-typed textarea satisfies locateQuote while guarding nothing — the guard's purpose is to point at real controlled-document text, not a sentence the user typed seconds ago. The one output carrying actual judgment (the model's 'this implies a new hazard' inference) is admittedly outside the guard.

5. Advisory severity 'capped at medium' (public-corpus) is a hand-picked tier masquerading as derived severity — constraint 5 requires derivation from citation frequency and harm linkage; 'we have no data so we chose medium' is freehand selection with an audit string. This branch is also net-new code (today severity is the 3-tier rarelyCited function) shipped without the harness to validate it.

6. Feature 2 is built on a premise the team's own research calls legally false ('publication is not dedication; there is no open industry best-practice corpus'), and the salvaged plan still ships FDA warning-letter-derived rules to a paying customer in Phase 3 while the ODbL license question is explicitly unresolved pending counsel.

7. Scope vs. reality: ~100 design-days (200-300 real) of graph databases, resolution passes, pg_trgm blocking, and Batches backfill — scale infrastructure for a 10k-document corpus that does not exist — for a 3-person team (one part-time student) with one lead and zero second customers. The one ask that serves winning customer #2 (feature 4, demo docs) is the least designed.

## Full critique

The strongest thing to say about this roadmap is that its own risk sections keep writing the verdict for me. The phrase "unmeasurable until the eval harness exists" appears, in slightly different words, in every one of the four designs. That is not four independent caveats — it is the same wall hit five times, and each design routes around it rather than through it. The correct engineering conclusion is the one none of the designs draw: the eval harness is not a co-requisite, it is the prerequisite, and none of Ben's three asks is it.

Sequencing is the fatal issue and it is not close. The founders' own README says precision is the entire asset because a false alarm costs credibility a missed finding does not. Today precision is unmeasured; the only quality signal is runAgreement, which is self-consistency, and it sits at ~71% at the ruleLevel — meaning roughly three findings in ten do not even reproduce run-to-run, before anyone asks whether they are correct. Every one of the three features increases output surface area: the graph adds absence findings, cumulative-risk adds change-gap findings, public-corpus adds a fifth rule source into the compliance pass. Each moves precision in a direction no one can see. Building any finding-generating feature on top of an unmeasured 71%-reproducible base is pouring new water into a bucket whose leak you have chosen not to measure. Whatever gets built first, it should be runEval.ts over the packages/eval fixtures that already exist. That is a days-to-weeks task, it is the highest-leverage thing on the board, and it is conspicuously absent from all four plans.

On which feature fails first in front of a real customer: the spider map. Not because the design is bad — graph-model and graph-ui are the most careful documents here — but because its data does not exist on day one for a new customer and its flagship output is structurally unshippable. The honest day-one edge set is conflicts_with (which only exists after a paid cross-check review with hand-enumerated related- record IDs) plus doc-ID regex references. A fresh customer who uploads their DHF has run zero reviews, so the "demoable day one" claim is true only for documents you already paid to review. Worse, the actual value — absence findings, "which risk control is unverified" — is precisely the output that breaks when a customer uploads 40 of 400 documents: every requirement looks orphaned, and you emit a storm of confident false alarms, the exact credibility-killer the README names. The corpus-completeness gate is the mitigation, and it is unmeasurable without the harness. So the map either renders empty (thin), renders a hairball (unreadable), or renders orphan findings that are wrong (credibility damage). The ghost-counterpart onboarding ring and the semantic-column Trace view are gorgeous meeting screenshots that assume clean canonical chains; real DHFs are merged-cell Excel. This is the most demo-ware-prone feature in the set: a screenshot machine whose production value is gated behind two things that do not exist (the edge extraction and the harness).

The anchoring guard is quietly weakened in the cumulative-risk design, and the design congratulates itself for it. Anchoring a change-fact to the engineer's just-typed proposal_text satisfies the mechanism — locateQuote succeeds — while guarding nothing. The guard's entire purpose is that a finding points at real, controlled document text the customer authored under a QMS; a verbatim match against a sentence the user typed thirty seconds ago is theater. "The model cannot invent a change the engineer didn't type" is true and irrelevant; the risk was never that the model invents the change, it is that the model's downstream risk inference is ungrounded, and that inference is exactly the "model-proposed consideration" the design admits it cannot anchor. So the one output that carries real judgment is the one output outside the guard, dressed in acceptance-gating.

Derived severity is bent in two places. The public-corpus design's advisory branch "caps at medium" for decision-document rules with no citation frequency — but "we have no frequency data so we chose medium" is a hand-picked tier, not a derivation from citation frequency and harm linkage. Constraint 5 says severity is derived from data; choosing a tier because you lack data is freehand selection with an audit string attached. The same cliff (LOW unless harm_linked is hand-set, then a jump to medium/high with nothing between) already afflicts every SOP rule and is not solved, only relabeled. Note also this branch does not exist in severity.ts today — the current model is the 3-tier rarelyCited function — so this is net-new severity code shipped without the harness to validate it.

Is Ben right? He is an advisor, and the validated pain came from a Fresenius Kabi quality leader and a regulatory expert, not from him. Rank his asks by whether they track that validated pain. Ask 3 (cumulative/continuous risk) is the real one: the catch-up-510(k) / letter-to-file-creep problem is documented, enforcement-backed, and a manufacturer with cleared devices and a change history feels it directly. But Ben's framing of it — a free-text box that returns the tool's "own judgment" of risk — is the single highest-liability output in the domain, and the design correctly refuses to build it and reframes to a deterministic ledger. Ask 1 (spider map) is Ben's aesthetic intuition: a visualization is not a pain. The research says plainly the map is a rendering of a graph, not the value, and the value (absence findings) is the part Ben did not ask for. Ask 2 (ingest online best practices) rests on a premise the team's own research demolishes — publication is not dedication, company docs are copyrighted, "there is no open industry best-practice corpus." The salvage (FDA-authored warning-letter frequency) is genuinely useful, but as narrow severity infrastructure for feature 3, not as a "section near SOPs," and there is no evidence the Fresenius lead wants it at all. Meanwhile the ODbL license tag on warning letters is flagged unresolved and the plan still ships public rules to a paying customer in Phase 3, before counsel sign-off — a licensing gamble taken knowingly.

Scope, finally. This is roughly 100 design-days of estimate (graph ~61d, cumulative ~24d, public-corpus ~12.5d) — realistically 200-300 real days — for three people, one of them an 18-year-old starting college, with one warm lead and zero second customers. The graph database, the resolution passes, pg_trgm blocking, and the Message Batches backfill are scale infrastructure for a 10k-document corpus that does not exist and has no path to existing. The one ask that directly serves the actual near-term goal — winning customer #2 — is feature 4 (synthetic demo documents), and it is the least designed, treated as an afterthought. That inversion is the scope story in one line: the roadmap invests most heavily in the feature least tied to survival and least in the feature most tied to it.
